I sat in Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport waiting to board my first ever international flight. Not only was this my first international flight, but it was also my first time flying alone.

"Group 7, you are now boarding," one of the flight attendants said over the loudspeaker.

*Here we go*. I swung my grey Eddie Bauer bag over my shoulder and squeezed into line, holding my passport and boarding ticket in my hand. I boarded the flight, and eventually we were off. Hours later, I gazed out of the airplane window while listening to *The View Between Villages - Extended Version* by Noah Kahan. The bright blue of the Caribbean Sea suddenly turned into the green folds of the mountains of Costa Rica.

From late December 2023 to mid-January 2024, I studied abroad in Monteverde, Costa Rica. This trip was through the Council on International Educational Exchange (CIEE), where I stayed on a small campus with other American college students in the remote green mountains of the northwestern part of the country. From the beaches of Manuel Antonio to the volcanoes of Parque Nacional Rincon de la Vieja, this experience was one of a lifetime.

image: /images/manuelantonio-cr.jpg

The J-Term cohort in my study abroad program was relatively small. There were four bungalows on campus, each with four rooms each. Each room housed four students, equipped with four beds, two wardrobes, four desks, and one shared bathroom with a sink, toilet, and shower. Our cohort filled up two of the bungalows and half of a third, and I roomed with two other girls so one of our bunks was empty.

image: /images/bungalow-outside-cr.jpg
image: /images/bungalow-inside-cr.jpg

The CIEE campus where our bungalows were located was amazing. The rec room was where everybody liked to hang out, which was equipped with a television, big fireplace, several couches, board games, and a foosball table. Almost none of the buildings were really *indoors*. Because the weather in Monteverde is pretty nice all year around (besides the unpredictable rainstorms due to being in the cloud forest), there were no doors on many of the buildings and several of the windows didn't have any glass and were instead just screens covered by bars. The walls of the rec room weren't really walls either, and instead they were large screens that went from the ground to the ceiling. Outside of the rec room was the basketball court, with a mini soccer field and volleyball field located nearby.

The basketball court holds some of my best memories from studying abroad in Monteverde. Almost every single night throughout my time in Costa Rica, our cohort would go to the basketball court, lay down on the asphalt, and look at the stars. The night sky was truly the most amazing thing I have ever seen, and it felt like I could see galaxies. To this day, whenever I see the night sky I think back to the nights laying on the basketball court trying to count the stars laughing alongside my new friends.

image: /images/stars-basketball-cr.jpg

Reception was another place our cohort liked to hang out. We would spend long afternoons watching the Monteverde sunset playing cards. Reception was also the spot where we would meet for classes or where we would go if the student life coordinators had to make an announcement. This area also connected the dining hall, student life coordinator offices, the computer lab, and the library. The dining hall was interesting because there were no phones allowed inside. Before each meal, we had to place our phones on a table outside the door before we headed in. This rule was weird to get used to at first, but I also think it's what allowed my cohort to bond so well because in the United States, we're all used to staring at our phones all the time, even while we eat.

image: /images/campus-cr.jpg

The campus farm was located a short walk from our bungalows. This was an *integrated farm*, or where systems work to benefit each other. This farm had many different forms of livestock, such as chickens and cows, and even had a system where the wastewater from campus was used as water for the crops, and then was recycled back to campus through a cleansing treatment.

I forgot to mention! While I was in Costa Rica I took a course called *Sustainable Development for the Tropics*. Our classrooms were small structures lined with long desks and wooden benches with a television in the front of the room. Like the rest of the buildings on campus, these classrooms had no doors and open windows.

image: /images/integrated-farm-cr.jpg
image: /images/classroom-cr.jpg

The CIEE Monteverde campus was located in a valley, and to get there from pretty much anywhere, we had to drive down pretty crazy mountain switchbacks. Sometimes the roads were gravel, but other times they were dirt. The roads were also pretty tight and there were no barriers on the side of the road protecting the vehicles from going over the side. To drive around, our cohort would go in these big white vans that could fit around twenty people, and the drivers had absolutely no fear driving down those mountain switchbacks! Because our campus was in the cloud forest, sometimes we would literally be driving above the clouds.

There was also a locally-managed park about a mile walk from campus, which was called Finca Ecológica San Luis. Throughout my time in Costa Rica, I would walk there with my cohort on our days off from class. We would meet a friendly older gentleman near the entrance, where would spend several minutes talking with him while he quizzed us on our Spanish. He seemed to enjoy speaking with us, and would help us correct our words whenever we mispronounced something. After a good hike through the forest, we would arrive at a small waterfall with a swimming area underneath.

image: /images/finca-ecologica-cr.jpg

During my sustainable development course, our professor made his best effort to teach us in the field. We visited many different places that I feel contributed greatly to my understanding of the topic. First, we visited the local town of Santa Elena, which was about a twenty minute van ride from campus. In Santa Elena, there were various coffee shops that got their coffee beans from local farms, street art by local artists (they also painted murals on the trash cans), and several green spaces throughout the town (such as a HUGE waterfall). Through visiting this town, we learned what exactly a tourism-based economy is and the pros and cons of the system. This was part of our first unit, sustainable tourism and ecotourism.

image: /images/santa-elena-cr.jpg

We also visited popular tourist destinations, such as Treetop, which is a park within the cloud forest the consists of several swinging bridges through the understory and canopy. We also visited a sustainability-certified hotel.

image: /images/treetopia-cr.jpg

After seeing the local coffee shops in Santa Elena, it was super interesting to visit a local coffee farm from which the coffee shops receive their products. We visited the coffee farm during our unit on sustainable versus conventional agriculture.

image: /images/coffee-farm-cr.jpg
image: /images/coffee-farm1-cr.jpg

As part of this unit, we also visited a regenerative, or sustainable, dairy farm. This farm was run by a family in Monteverde, called Finca Bello Amanecer. Regenerative agriculture is an outcome-based food production system that protects and restores soil health, protects the climate, water resources, and biodiversity, and enhances the quality of the food produced. It was interesting to walk around the farm and see first-hand how the farmers minimize soil disturbance, diversify their crops, integrate livestock, and reduce the need of biological and chemical inputs all in an effort to reduce their impact.

image: /images/dairy-farm-cr.jpg

While this wasn't necessarily part of the course I took, the student life coordinators organized a trip to El Tigre Waterfalls. This was a great time to connect with others and explore the natural wonders of the country. The waterfalls were huge!!

image: /images/el-tigre-cr.jpg
image: /images/waterfall.jpg

During our last unit of the course, renewable energy, our class took a two-day trip to Parque Nacional Rincón de la Vieja. On our way to the park, we stopped by a few renewable energy plants, such as a hydropower plant, a wind farm, and a geothermal plant at the base of Miravalles Volcano in Guanacaste.

image: /images/renewable-energy-cr.jpg
image: /images/volcano-cr.jpg

After a four hour drive from Monteverde, we made it to Parque Nacional Rincón de la Vieja! Rincón de la Vieja is the largest and most active volcano in Costa Rica, and unrest was reported during our visit. Miravalles is a stratovolcano located nearby and is also active, although its last recorded eruption was in 1946.

image: /images/volcano1-cr.jpg

After our courses ended but before our final exam and presentations began, my cohort decided to take a weekend trip to Manuel Antonia, which is a beach and national park located on the Pacific coast. It was cool getting to experience the coastal area of Costa Rica, and I saw many different animals, such as sloths and iguanas, that I couldn't see back in Monteverde.

image: /images/manuel-antonio-beach-cr.jpg

Sadly, my time in Costa Rica came to an end in three short weeks, but it felt like a lifetime.

In a short period of time, I made great friendships and formed a bond to a country I could have only dreamed of being able to visit one day. And I did so while gaining a better understanding of sustainable development, contributing to my learning as an environmental studies major hoping to one day work in the climate change sphere.
